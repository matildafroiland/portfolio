weather data, made useful ↓

An Android app that shows homeowners how much wind and heavy rain their home has faced, and what they can do to protect it.

My role: the only designer on the team Team: 5 developers + me When: about 12 weeks, spring 2026 Methods: survey, guerrilla testing, usability testing, co-design
the problem

Storms and heavy rain are getting more common, but homeowners have no simple way to see what their own address has been through.

what we did

Built an Android app on open data from the Norwegian Meteorological Institute (MET), shaped by a survey, guerrilla tests and a usability test.

the result

Search an address, see its wind and rain history in plain language, and get concrete measures you can filter.

good to know

IN2000 is a software engineering course, not a design course. We were mostly graded on code, architecture and teamwork, so design got less time than I would have liked. Many design decisions were made quickly, based on what we had time for.

a change of plans

Week 12: the case changed

In week 12, MET changed the case description, and our target group no longer fit. We lost two and a half weeks of work: a survey, an interview and the analysis. The MVP was due a week and a half later.

Hikers and avalanche risk

one week to change direction

New target group, new data sources, a new survey (no time for interviews), and a working MVP.

homeowners, wind and heavy rain

The direction we built for the rest of the project.

what it taught me

Pick the fastest method that still gives real answers. A survey was the only research that fit into one week.

and

Losing work is frustrating. Talking about it openly as a team got us moving again faster than pushing through.

also

Working in short sprints made the change manageable. We re-planned in one sprint, so the rest of the project didn't suffer as much as it could have.

research

Our assumption was wrong

We thought homeowners would care most about floods and landslides.

I planned, ran and analysed the survey (with help from the team). The answers pointed somewhere else: people wanted to know about wind and heavy rain, so that became the focus of the whole app.

  • Strong wind50%
  • Heavy rain46%
  • Flooding30%
  • Landslides30%

Share of survey respondents who rated each topic "quite" or "very" important for their home.

a design decision

We said no to the feature users asked for most

The survey showed people wanted a colour-coded risk map. We worked on it for a long time, but we couldn't calculate risk levels reliably, and MET agreed that a homeowner mostly cares about their own plot. A red area on a map feels like a fact.

Showing a risk colour we couldn't stand behind would have been worse than showing none. We used plain-language text and graphs instead, which users had also asked for.

testing

One user, three tasks, a lot learned

After guerrilla tests around campus, I ran a moderated usability test with think-aloud. The user had to find information about her own address, and I timed each task.

working across disciplines

One designer, five developers

I owned the design work, the developers owned the backend and architecture, and we met in the middle. They joined research and testing, and I wrote code too. It meant I had to explain design decisions in a way developers could build from, and learn enough of their world to know what was realistic.

What the team built it with:

Kotlin, Jetpack Compose, MVVM, MET Frost API, Mapbox, Figma and Scrumban.

my role

I was the only designer on the team and was involved in the whole project. These were my parts:

  • All the Figma design: screens, flows and sketches
  • Planned, ran and analysed the research, with help
  • Crazy 8 session and the moderated usability test
  • Coded the FAQ page and the welcome screen
if I did it again

I'd do more research at the start, and test before we implemented everything. Even a quick test of the Figma prototype would have caught the easy mistakes, like the hidden card, before they were built.

what I learned

Design moves at a different speed than development. Features were often built before I could test them. Next time I'd agree from day one on when we test and who signs off on design decisions.
